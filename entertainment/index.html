Paramount–Warner Bros.: the $110B deal California is trying to kill CONFIRMED

Fri Sep 11, evening·Strongest sources: LA County DEO final report (via TheWrap, Fox LA); MarketWatch

What happened

  • A final LA County report (CVL Economics, for the County Department of Economic Opportunity and Film Office) estimates ~4,500 direct film/TV jobs at risk from the merger, plus 10,360 total job-years, $4.06B in business output, and $1.26B in wages at risk. CONFIRMED
  • The DOJ cleared the deal June 12, but California AG Rob Bonta and 11 other state AGs plus the WGA sued to block it. Under a July 24 stipulation the companies cannot close until after a trial set for March 2027. CONFIRMED
  • Paramount's agreement includes a ticking fee of 25¢/share per quarter past Sept 30 — about $650M per quarter of delay. CONFIRMED
  • Paramount is now floating relocating out of California; one report estimates a $10–20B statewide economic hit. HOLD — reported today, single-report sourcing, and at least one antitrust lawyer calls it pressure tactics.

Why it matters

  • This is the largest media merger ever attempted. Approval redraws the studio map (Paramount+ and Max under one roof, one theatrical slate); a block keeps two weakened companies burning cash separately.

Second order

  • If it closes: one buyer for fewer films — Paramount promised "at least 30 films a year," which is a cut from two slates' combined output. Talent, vendors, and theater owners all lose leverage.
  • If it stays blocked: Paramount pays ~$650M per quarter in ticking fees while fighting 12 states — delay itself becomes the punishment.

Watch

  • Friday's settlement-conference scheduling call; whether California and Paramount reach any resolution "in the coming weeks." A real relocation filing would confirm the threat is more than leverage.

Why now

  • The September 30 ticking-fee clock starts in 19 days. Every delay now has a price tag — which is exactly why the pressure campaign is peaking this week.

Sources (primary first): LA County DEO final report via TheWrap and Fox LA; ticking-fee terms via MarketWatch; relocation reporting via Washington Examiner (single report — held).

Two movies carried the biggest summer in box-office history CONFIRMED

Fri Sep 11, evening·Strongest sources: Rentrak (via EW); Box Office Mojo (via A.V. Club); Rotten Tomatoes

What happened

  • Spider-Man: Brand New Day held #1 domestically for six straight weeks — $18.2M over Labor Day weekend, ~$923M domestic, $2.4B worldwide. CONFIRMED
  • It sits ~$13.6M behind Star Wars: The Force Awakens for the all-time domestic record and could pass it this week. CONFIRMED
  • The Odyssey retook #1 globally ($45.9M vs. $44.1M), reaching $1.6B — the most commercially successful film of Christopher Nolan's career and #14 all-time worldwide. CONFIRMED
  • Rentrak: 2026 is now the highest-grossing domestic summer on record. CONFIRMED
  • Practical Magic 2 (Bullock/Kidman) opens this weekend; Boxoffice Pro projects ~$40M and the end of Spider-Man's streak. HOLD — a projection, not a result. Actuals land Monday.

Why it matters

  • The "theaters are dead" story is wrong in an important way: theatrical isn't dead, it's concentrated. Two films carried the best summer ever. That concentration is the business story.

Second order

  • Studios chase fewer, bigger bets — which further squeezes the mid-budget theatrical film. Exhibitors win on volume but become hostage to the tentpole calendar: a thin Q4 without a giant is a bad quarter, full stop.
  • Third order: if two films can decide whether a summer is "record" or "disaster," greenlight math gets even more risk-averse — and original IP gets even harder to finance theatrically.

Watch

  • Monday actuals: does Practical Magic 2 hit ~$40M? A female-targeted legacy sequel outperforming would complicate the "only superheroes travel" read.

Why now

  • The streak ends this weekend either way — Spider-Man is one weekend from the all-time domestic crown, and its challenger arrives today. This is the handoff week.

Sources: A.V. Club / Box Office Mojo; EW; Rotten Tomatoes; EW / Rentrak; projection via Page Six (held).

What the headlines miss

Two things the coverage is underweighting

1. The ticking fee is the real deadline

Every story frames March 2027 as the decision point. The money says otherwise: ~$650M per quarter past September 30. Paramount is negotiating against a meter, not a calendar — which reframes the relocation threat as a negotiating tactic with a very expensive clock behind it.

2. Warner Bros. has no $100M domestic film in 2026

Page Six notes WB enters Q4 without a single domestic $100M grosser — the first time since 2001 — after a 2025 slate (Sinners, A Minecraft Movie, One Battle After Another) that worked. The headline is the merger; the signal is that one of the two merging studios is having its worst commercial year in a quarter-century during the best box-office summer ever. That's weakness meeting leverage.
